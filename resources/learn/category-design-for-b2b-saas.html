How to Build a Category Design Strategy for B2B SaaS in 90 Days

Category design is the process of naming and defining a new market space so your product becomes the obvious reference point in it, instead of competing feature-by-feature inside a category someone else already owns.

The short answer

Category design is the strategic work of defining a new problem space — naming it, framing the old way of solving it as inadequate, and positioning your product as the natural answer to that new frame. Done well, buyers stop comparing you feature-by-feature against incumbents and start evaluating you as the reference point for a category you effectively created.

It’s the opposite of competing on a features checklist inside a category your buyer already has fixed opinions about. If you’re “another X, but better,” you’re playing a game you can only win by being cheaper or slightly faster — a weak position for most early-stage B2B SaaS companies.

Why most B2B SaaS companies skip this step

Category design is easy to skip because it doesn’t feel like an immediate task — there’s no ticket for “define your category,” while there’s always a ticket for “ship the next feature” or “run another campaign.” Most founders back into positioning by copying language from whichever competitor is winning the most deals, which guarantees you stay a slightly-worse version of them in the buyer’s mind.

The companies that skip this step don’t fail immediately — they just plateau: every deal becomes a bake-off, every sales cycle involves justifying why you’re different, and marketing spend goes toward defending a position instead of building one.

The 90-day framework, at a high level

Days 1–30: Diagnose the old category. Identify exactly what buyers currently believe the “old way” is, and where it demonstrably breaks down for your ICP. This isn’t guesswork — pull it from win/loss interviews, support tickets, and sales call transcripts, not internal opinion.

Days 31–60: Name and frame the new category. Give the new way a name a buyer can say out loud and repeat to their boss. The frame needs three things: a clear “before” (the old, broken way), a clear “after” (the new way, embodied by your category), and a reason the shift is happening now (a market, technology, or buyer-behavior change that makes the old way obsolete).

Days 61–90: Prove it in market. Ship the category language across your highest-visibility surfaces — homepage, sales deck, one flagship piece of content — and test it in real sales conversations. Category design isn’t finished at a whiteboard; it’s validated when prospects start repeating your framing back to you unprompted.

The most common mistake

Confusing category design with a tagline. A clever name without the underlying “old way vs. new way” argument is just branding — it won’t survive a skeptical buyer’s first pushback. The name is the last 10% of the work; the diagnostic argument for why the old category fails is the other 90%.

How to know it’s working

You’ll see three concrete signals: prospects start using your category language unprompted in discovery calls, competitors begin repositioning against your frame instead of the reverse, and sales cycles shorten because the conversation starts from “which vendor in this new category” instead of “why should I switch from what I already use.”

Category design compounds with everything else in a GTM system — content, AEO, outbound — because it gives all of it a consistent argument to repeat, instead of every asset making its own disconnected case for why you’re worth a look.
